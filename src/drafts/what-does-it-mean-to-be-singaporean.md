## **Being Singaporean: Transcending Ethnic Identities**

What does it mean to be Singaporean? What does it mean to be a Singaporean Christian? This article examines these issues in two parts. Firstly, we examine how Singapore has grappled with its cultural identities. This is viewed through the lens of the evolving immigrant across three eras from Singapore’s past. Next, we explore what the Singaporean Christian has to offer Singaporeans in living out his/her spiritual identity in the midst of his/her cultural heritage. 

## Part 1: The Evolution of the Immigrant 

How can Singapore transcend its ethnic identities to be truly Singaporean? In short, there are three options, exemplified through the three eras in Singapore’s history: 1) Keep Fighting; 2) Keep Helping; and 3) Keep Remaking.

### **Stage 1: 1800s - Keep Fighting**

**1800s: Clash of Identities – Feuds and Friendly Foes amongst Immigrants**

Singapore has always been a cultural melting pot since its inception as an entrepôt port in the 1800s. Back then, there was no “Singaporean” identity as such. The collective identity of Singaporeans, if they could be called as such, was bound by geography and their shared experience of their Diaspora.

Migrants originating from India, China and other parts of the world were forced to work and co-exist together in a tight land mass 50km by 26km in dimensions. Competition and the desire for a better life brought this wave of migrants from their homelands. In this initial chaos, people of the same ethnic and ancestral home villages banded together in clans and associations to advance common economic aims. Their desire was to preserve their own ethnicity in the face of cultural and physical encroachment from other groups. Their cultural struggle was learning to co-exist without impinging upon other groups. Friendly and not so friendly competition characterized this era.

### **Stage 2: 1900s - Keep Helping **

**Convergence of Identities – Family Spirit emerging amongst immigrants**

Over time, the notion of identity gradually turned from the commonalities of geographical origins to the commonalities of geographic communes. A new generation born and bred in the tight commune of Singapore learnt to co-exist as a collective community through the birth pains of a budding nation.

During this stage, those born and bred in Singapore genuinely called themselves Singaporeans. Yet even they questioned whether they were first Chinese/Malay/Indian or simply Singaporeans. The real glue for cohesion was the commonality of suffering together.

As Singapore endured the trials and adversity of war, economic and political crises, man-made disasters and the like, the distinct ethnic communities in Singapore learnt that adversity is no respecter of one’s origin. In order to overcome adversity, one has to set aside differences and band together as a family. It became less important whether one was Chinese, Malay or Indian. Rather, one learnt to look out for each other as family members of the same “kampong”. Indeed, Singapore was a better place because of this “kampong” spirit.

### **Stage 3: 2000 - Keep Remaking**

#### **Creation of New identities**

Singapore in the 2000s was the regional hub and gateway for trade and business. There was greater connectivity brought about by a world class port, airport, and the Internet. With a strong and stable government and healthy economic growth, Singapore was highly exposed to new ideas, identities and ideologies. As a result, she became a desired landing spot for talented and mobile immigrants. As Singaporeans contributed to Singapore's success, a sense of ownership, even entitlement, arose amongst the citizens of Singapore. 

#### **Something new, something old **

In this third stage of Singapore’s history, there was a potent mix of the old and the new. The settled immigrants began to explore what it meant to be uniquely Singaporean. They pondered over what the national dress was. They discussed the merits for and against Singlish, and debated over who was “Singaporean” enough to lead Singapore. At the same time, they celebrated National Day with a true sense of pride and belonging. Regarding the influx of new immigrants, the now-settled immigrants argued over whether the new immigrant should do NS. They railed at how these new Chinese/Indian/Others jarred us so in our hawker centres and other public places. Occasionally, they railed against the immigrants for robbing us of our jobs, and taking over whole enclaves for their own. 

During this stage, the old and the new immigrants struggled to mix. Success, if any, was experienced in parts. Some subscribed to the mindset of the survival of the fittest and kept up with the friendly competition. Others found communities and a new “kampong” spirit. There was a blending of identity, as the new sought to fit into the evolving Singaporean psyche. To this day, there appears to be no real settled solution except to keep mixing and to keep moving ahead.

## Part 2: The Transformation of the Spiritual Sojourner

Singapore today continues to demonstrate Stage 1 Competitiveness, Stage 2 Familial Connectivity, and Stage 3 Movement. In fact, Singaporeans today do not need to be in Singapore to feel Singaporean. Many Singaporeans today travel more widely. They have second homes outside of Singapore, and have friends and relatives not living in Singapore. Singaporeans today are sojourners on a larger journey. For the sake of economic and emotional wellbeing, they are happy to call Singapore home while making a home elsewhere.

### The double identity of the Singaporean Christian

In this regard, the Singaporean Christian has something to offer that may be instructive for Singaporeans today. For the Singaporean Christian, the heart of the matter involves the matter of the heart. Where one heart is, there one’s treasure would be also. 

### National and spiritual identities

For the Christ-Follower, his heart and identity is in Christ. Consequently, he lives out a spiritual reality that transcends the physical. His blended reality infuses the material, physical, social and economic fabric of life with a tinge of the divine.

The Christ-Follower recognizes that he/she is on a different journey. He is headed towards a destination that is ironically unreachable while one still has breath on this good earth. The journey compels the Christ-Follower to find what Thomas Kelly refers to as one’s Divine Centre in an invisible God. IT demands that he serves fellow human beings and consider others better than himself. To follow Christ is to live a life of simplicity in recognition of the transience of living on earth.

### Uniqueness of the spiritual identity

Such a Christ-Follower does not strive for self-focus. He does not make distinctions between “us” and “them”. Neither does he invests in false roots while in transit on Earth. Instead, he welcomes the presence of other fellow sojourners, not as intrusion but as an invitation to journey together. He seeks to co-create impactful moments while on this momentary journey on earth. He learns to tune out the temporary disruptions of living together for the sake of striving together for a greater cause. 

One should not confuse such spirituality with religiosity. The former is alive and adaptive while the latter is bound by codes and creeds. The former seeks to live out while the latter makes demands that one must live by. Religion, enforced by flawed humanity, is ultimately also flawed. Spirituality, founded upon a relationship with a living God, is salvation for flawed humanity.

## The Final Word: Being Spiritual

For the Singapore, being Singaporean can mean that one continues on the current vacillations between fighting, family or flux. A better way is simply to be more spiritual. How well Singaporeans can emulate the Christ-Follower life without Christ as centre is a matter of further deliberation. Nonetheless, the direction to move into is definitely inward and upward, so as to advance forward and outward. At least for the start of this new direction, the Christ-Follower should point the way to Singaporeans, just as Paul did so for the Athenians.
