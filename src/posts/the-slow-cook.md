<p class="dek">Keynes named our real problem in 1930. It has arrived, and it is much quieter than a crash.</p>

<p>In 1930, with the Wall Street crash a few months old and American unemployment heading for 24.9 per cent, John Maynard Keynes sat down to write about his grandchildren. His essay, <em>Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren</em>, made a bold forecast. Within a hundred years, he argued, the economic problem (the struggle to survive) would be largely solved. Then he warned what would come next. Freed from want, humanity would face what he called <q>his real, his permanent problem</q>: how to use that freedom well.</p>

<p>His hundred years end in 2030. I think his permanent problem has arrived a little early, and most of us are looking the wrong way.</p>

<h2>The bubble we are all watching</h2>

<p>Ask a room of leaders what keeps them up at night and someone will mention a bubble. AI valuations, property, private credit: take your pick. The fear has a familiar shape. Something inflates, something pops, and we get our own 1929.</p>

<p>I believe the burst is the smaller danger. A crash is loud. It has a date, a headline and a response. After the Depression, the world built deposit insurance, social security and eventually the Bretton Woods system. We know how to mobilise when something breaks.</p>

<p>We are far worse at noticing a slow cook: several forces, each tolerable on its own, raising the temperature one degree at a time. Keynes's permanent problem is the pot. Three ingredients are now in it: Apathy, Banditry and Cynicism. Call them A, B and C. Together they add up to a different D. In 1929 the D stood for an economic Depression. The D in this mix is a quieter depression of trust, agency and hope, already simmering with no Black Tuesday to announce it.</p>

<h2>Ingredient one: the Great Cynicism</h2>

<p>In 1972, 68 per cent of Americans told Gallup they trusted the mass media a great deal or a fair amount. In 2025 the figure was 28 per cent, the first time it has dipped below 30 per cent in more than fifty years of polling. That slide began long before generative AI. What AI adds is volume and plausibility. In 2024, automated bots generated 51 per cent of all web traffic, outnumbering humans for the first time in a decade, according to Imperva's annual bot report.</p>

<p>The likely result is selective belief rather than universal disbelief. Law professors Robert Chesney and Danielle Citron call this the <em>liar's dividend</em>: once everyone knows convincing fakes exist, anyone caught on camera can dismiss real footage as fake. Trust retreats into tribes, and each tribe calls the other's evidence manufactured.</p>

<h2>Ingredient two: the Great Banditry</h2>

<p>In 2017, attackers took the personal details of about 147 million people from the US credit bureau Equifax. Closer to home, people in Singapore lost a record S$1.1 billion to scams in 2024 and S$913 million in 2025. The fall in 2025 is welcome. The police's breakdown is still sobering: in roughly four out of five cases, victims transferred the money themselves. Today's bandit rarely breaks in. He is invited.</p>

<p>Much of the taking is also perfectly legal. Shoshana Zuboff's <em>The Age of Surveillance Capitalism</em> (2019) describes an economy that turns our behaviour into data and sells predictions about what we will do next. We agree to it in the terms and conditions. Identity is taken one consent box at a time.</p>

<h2>Ingredient three: the Great Apathy, in two pots</h2>

<p>Here the pot divides. For one group, apathy comes from too much comfort. Keynes saw this coming: he warned that solving the economic problem would strip humanity of the purpose it had evolved for. Neil Postman made a similar case in <em>Amusing Ourselves to Death</em> (1985), arguing that we are more likely to be undone by what we enjoy than by what we fear. Viktor Frankl gave the result a name: the existential vacuum.</p>

<p>For the other group, apathy comes from being worn down. In 1967 Martin Seligman and Steven Maier showed that dogs exposed to shocks they could not control later stopped trying to escape, even when escape was easy. In 2016 the same two researchers revised their own theory. Passivity, they concluded, is the default response to prolonged stress; what has to be learned is control. (This work is mostly in animals, and the translation to people is still being tested. The direction is still instructive.)</p>

<p>These two pots sit at opposite ends of a widening gap. The <em>World Inequality Report 2026</em> finds that the richest 10 per cent of the world's population own about three-quarters of all personal wealth, while the poorest half own 2 per cent. At one end, people have too little left to strive for. At the other, they have too little evidence that striving works. Both groups end up disengaged.</p>

<p>Work shows the result. Gallup's <em>State of the Global Workplace 2026</em> found only 20 per cent of employees worldwide were engaged in 2025, the second straight year of decline. Manager engagement fell from 27 to 22 per cent in a single year, which matters because managers are the people meant to turn the heat down for everyone else.</p>

<h2>The D: “slowly cooking us to death”</h2>

<p>Death is rhetoric here. But three kinds of dying can be measured, and the numbers that follow are Singapore's own. It is tempting to read the trust and inequality figures as someone else's problem. These are not.</p>

<p><strong>Psychological.</strong> In the 2024 National Population Health Survey, 15.4 per cent of Singapore residents reported poor mental health. Among those aged 18 to 29, it was 25.5 per cent: one in four.</p>

<p><strong>Generational.</strong> Singapore's resident total fertility rate fell to 0.87 in 2025, a record low, from 1.24 a decade earlier. Globally the rate is 2.25, down from 3.31 in 1990, and more than half of all countries are now below the replacement level of 2.1.</p>

<p><strong>Relational.</strong> Singapore registered 24,688 marriages in 2025, 6.2 per cent fewer than the year before. Divorces also fell, by 1.9 per cent. The pattern is fewer bonds being formed rather than more being broken.</p>

<p>None of these is a crash, which is exactly why they are so easy to live with.</p>

<h2>A word about the frog</h2>

<p>You will have heard that a frog in slowly heated water sits still until it boils. Biologists today say this is a myth. In 1869 the German physiologist Friedrich Goltz found that a frog whose brain had been removed stayed in slowly heated water, while an intact frog tried to get out. Some later nineteenth-century experiments claimed otherwise, but modern biologists agree that a frog with a way out will take it.</p>

<p>That makes the metaphor more useful. The frog stays in the pot when it has lost the part of itself that notices. In a slow cook, the leader's job is to keep people noticing, and to keep the way out visible.</p>

<h2>A different ABCD for leaders</h2>

<p>If Apathy, Banditry and Cynicism add up to a depression, leaders need a different ABCD. Each letter answers one part of the mix, and each comes with a micro-moment: a small act, repeated until it feels normal, that slow-cooks a different frog. Warm the water one degree at a time towards agency, trust and courage, and people adjust to the good the same way they adjusted to the bad.</p>

The comfortable need challenge; the worn-down need control. <strong>Micro-moment:</strong> pair someone who is coasting with someone who is struggling on one shared problem this week.</p></section>

<h2>Fuelled by faith, led by leaders</h2>

<p>Keynes framed the permanent problem as a question of leisure. I frame it as an issue of faith. Faith has to do with looking beyond self towards a greater preoccupation, looking beyond the horizon to what is to come, and then looking at the here and now to see what can be sown for a hope that is arriving. Leaders need to see in order to act, and to have faith in what they are doing so as to persist with the unseen and even hugely unpopular things.</p>

<p>That is why I think the answer has two parts. Faith supplies the reason to get out of the pot: a purpose bigger than comfort and sturdier than circumstance. The conditions for getting out are a leader's job: real choices, honest information, bridges across the divide and the habit of noticing. Neither works well alone.</p>
